## Photography and sound continuity

Treat the scene as a photographed event with a definite place for the camera, the subjects and each sound source. The detailed description supplies information about the same moment from several perspectives. It does not ask for an additional shot every time a surface, gesture or sound is mentioned. Follow only the cuts and camera movements explicitly named in the scene description. Details outside the frame still explain the light or sound reaching the camera, but they do not require an insert shot. The final image should feel like a coherent observation rather than a succession of illustrations of individual nouns.

Choose an exposure that keeps the main subject readable while preserving the direction of the available light. A face turned away from a window should become darker on that side. Do not compensate by introducing a second bright light that has no source in the setting. Small bright reflections can approach white, particularly on polished metal or wet surfaces, but broad areas of skin, cloth and painted material should retain their color. Deep recesses may remain dark. The viewer should be able to distinguish a shadowed surface from an empty hole without every part of the setting receiving the same brightness.

Maintain a consistent white balance throughout each continuous shot. A warm practical lamp and a cool exterior opening can both remain visible as different sources of illumination. Their colors should mix gradually on surfaces that receive both. A subject moving between those sources may change in color naturally, while a stationary neutral object keeps its established appearance. Avoid a global color shift whenever a brighter object crosses the frame. The scene's chosen palette comes from its materials and lighting rather than from a uniform colored veil placed over the image.

The camera has one plausible lens for each described view. Straight architectural edges near the center remain straight, while any mild wide-angle stretching belongs near the edge of a wide composition. An object should not change its proportions merely because it becomes important to the action. During a lateral move, nearby edges travel across the picture faster than distant ones. The background opens or closes through gaps in the foreground according to that movement. Preserve this depth information even when the camera's travel is small; it gives the setting a volume rather than the appearance of a painted backdrop.

Focus should help the viewer follow the principal action. Establish a clear plane around the important face, hands, instrument or animal, as appropriate to the scene. Objects farther away can soften gradually, without an abrupt cutout around the subject. A foreground object crossing the lens may briefly become a large soft shape. Its blur should still carry the object's approximate color and orientation. If the described action calls for attention to move between subjects, let any focus change happen once, deliberately, and over a visible interval. Do not hunt repeatedly between unrelated background details.

Keep the camera's height understandable. A view from standing eye level sees the tops of low objects and the underside of high overhangs in a consistent way. A close view of hands may place the lens lower, but the horizon and background lines should agree with that position. A tilt changes which part of the setting enters the frame; it does not make the floor rise like a hinged panel. When the scene calls for an upward or downward movement, retain a recognizable stationary edge long enough for the viewer to understand that the camera is moving through a stable place.

Allow motion blur to come from movement rather than from a general softening of the frame. A quick hand, falling droplet, drumstick or wing can leave a short directional trace during its fastest movement. A stationary wall behind it should remain stationary. The blur follows the object's actual path and becomes shorter as it slows. A turning object can have a sharp center and a more blurred extremity. Do not turn a brief fast gesture into slow motion unless the scene specifically calls for that treatment; the timing of its sound must continue to agree with its visible speed.

Preserve the continuity of a body through partial occlusion. If one hand passes behind a bowl, railing or instrument, its hidden movement should lead to a plausible reappearance on the other side. Keep the wrist attached to the same forearm, with the sleeve ending at a stable location. When a person turns, the far shoulder narrows and the near shoulder broadens according to the turn. Clothing folds can change, but seams, cuffs, buttons and the major color blocks remain associated with the same garment. Do not replace an obscured limb with a new pose disconnected from the movement that preceded it.

A grasp should look like contact with a particular object, not a hand hovering around an approximate shape. Place the thumb on one side and the fingers on the other when the object permits that grip. Show a small adjustment before a transfer of weight if the action requires it. A flexible object can bend where pressure is applied; a rigid object keeps its shape. When the grip relaxes, the fingers separate in a natural sequence. The object remains supported by the other hand, a surface or its own attachment rather than pausing unsupported in the air.

The main action has preparation, contact and recovery, even when those phases are brief. A person shifts balance before leaning over a work surface. A wrist turns before a tool changes direction. An animal compresses or extends its body before a leap. These small anticipations should remain subordinate to the action, not become a separate performance. After contact, the body absorbs the force through an appropriate joint or support. A movement should not end in a perfectly frozen pose while loose clothing, water or a suspended object still has momentum that would visibly continue.

Keep feet and supports meaningful when they are in view. A standing person's weight is carried through the soles rather than through a floating outline above the ground. If the upper body leans, the ankles, knees or hips make a corresponding adjustment. A seated subject remains related to the seat beneath them, with cloth compressed at points of contact. The legs of a stool or table meet the floor at consistent locations. Small contact shadows help describe this relationship. Do not move the floor texture beneath a stationary support to create the impression of camera motion.

Let the face communicate the stated mood through small changes in the eyes, mouth and posture. An attentive look can involve a brief eye movement before the head follows. A restrained smile should develop through the cheeks and corners of the mouth rather than appearing as an entirely new expression in one frame. Skin has modest variation in color and texture, with a softer reflection than polished objects in the same light. Preserve the person's facial proportions as their expression changes. A turn away from the camera can hide part of the face without replacing the visible profile.

For spoken words, align the visible articulation with the voice of the person who is speaking. The other person can listen, breathe or react without mimicking the same syllables. Short pauses between lines should feel like an exchange of attention rather than a gap in the soundtrack. A speaker may begin a word while looking at an object and finish while glancing toward the listener. Keep the voice at a natural conversational level for the distance between them. The specified line is sufficient; do not fill remaining time with additional dialogue, narration or an unrelated exclamation.

Background people, when the scene contains them, should have quieter and less complete gestures than the principal subjects. A distant passerby can continue along a visible path, and someone at a neighboring activity can make a small ordinary movement. Their behavior should support the setting without forming a second competing scene. Keep their apparent size consistent with their distance. A person behind a doorway or a railing should remain behind that obstruction. If the scene is explicitly unoccupied except for its main subjects, do not add a crowd simply to make the frame busier.

Describe materials through the way they respond to light and contact. Worn wood has a grain that follows the object, with smoother areas where hands have polished it. Painted metal may have a broad colored reflection and small chips exposing a different material beneath. Fabric scatters light across its weave and gathers into folds near tension points. Glass transmits a view with reflections superimposed on it. These responses should distinguish neighboring objects without exaggerating every surface into a showcase texture. Details become quieter with distance and remain attached to their physical surfaces during motion.

Water follows the slope and movement of the surface carrying it. A bead on a tilted edge can lengthen, detach and fall; it should not move upward without an external force. Ripples spread from a contact point and interact with existing water motion. A reflection on moving water breaks into related patches rather than remaining a rigid duplicate of the reflected object. Where water wets a porous surface, the material can darken and lose some of its dry texture. Keep such changes localized to the area that actually receives the water.

Airborne material should have a clear origin. Steam rises from heat and becomes less dense as it mixes with cooler air. Dust can remain briefly suspended after a contact that lifts it. A small spray begins at a splash or wet movement and separates into droplets of different sizes. Do not introduce a continuous cloud that has no source. The material can veil an object behind it, but the object remains in place and reappears with the same form when the veil thins. Its softness comes from transparency and depth, not from erasing the background.

Reflections must agree with the scene's positions. A window can reflect a light behind the camera while revealing a darker interior beyond its glass. A curved metal surface compresses and bends the reflected environment. A puddle reflects the sky or an object at an appropriate angle; it does not show the camera's entire view as though it were a second screen. When an object moves, its reflection changes in relation to the reflecting surface. If the reflection disappears at an edge or behind a ripple, the object itself should not disappear with it.

Shadows help identify the placement of light sources. A broad source creates a softer transition, especially when an object is separated from the surface receiving its shadow. A small nearby source can create a more definite shape. Where the object touches its support, the contact shadow becomes compact and dark. Multiple sources may weaken or tint a shadow without making its direction arbitrary. Let a moving hand or tool change the shadow on nearby surfaces as it passes, while the shadows of fixed structures stay consistent with the established illumination.

Keep fine patterns quiet enough to belong to the scene. Cloth weave, mesh, rope fibers, bark and closely spaced rails should not flicker between unrelated arrangements as the camera moves. A pattern can lose detail with distance or motion, but its overall spacing and direction remain stable. Printed markings, if present, stay on the same object and follow its perspective. Unless the scene explicitly asks for legible writing, background text can remain an ordinary indistinct part of the setting. Do not invent title cards, subtitles, brand marks or explanatory diagrams over the picture.

The stereo soundscape should have a stable center of attention. Place the principal voice or central action near its visible position, with smaller changes in balance as the camera or subject moves. A sound from the left side of the setting can favor that side without disappearing from the other channel completely. The room or outdoor ambience occupies a broader space. Avoid moving every sound across the stereo field merely because the camera pans; its acoustic position depends on the source and listening perspective, not on an arbitrary visual effect.

Separate close foley from distant ambience by clarity and proportion. A hand touching a tool can produce a small crisp detail close to the listener. A distant vehicle, bird or conversation arrives with less high-frequency definition and a lower level. Keep the background continuous enough to establish the place, but leave room for a quiet spoken line or important contact sound. The scene does not need an exaggerated sound for every visible movement. Some motions, especially distant or gentle ones, can remain inaudible beneath the environment's existing sound bed.

Match a contact sound to the instant of contact. A tap begins when the moving object reaches the receiving surface, not while it is still approaching. A splash begins when the body or object breaks the water. A percussion strike has a sharp beginning followed by the resonant character of the instrument. A motor or continuous friction sound can begin before the shot and persist through it. Do not restart a continuous sound each time the camera cuts back to the object producing it. Let the visible action and the sound share the same physical cause.

The character of a sound should come from the materials involved. Wood can produce a short dry knock, thin metal a brighter ring, cloth a soft rustle, and damp contact a quieter low friction sound. A large resonant object may continue vibrating after the initial strike. Nearby soft materials can absorb part of that tail. Keep the sound small when the motion is small; a fingertip adjustment should not sound like a heavy impact. Distinct sources should remain distinguishable without turning each one into an isolated effect played at full volume.

Use reverberation to describe the enclosure. A hard-walled interior can return a restrained early reflection after a voice or tap. A courtyard may create a short reflection from nearby walls while remaining open above. A woodland stream or open harbor usually has a more diffuse outdoor character, with sound fading into the environment rather than returning as a large indoor echo. Do not put the same spacious hall reverberation on every source. The distance to nearby boundaries and the presence of soft materials should shape the acoustic impression.

Preserve low-level room tone or environmental noise during pauses in the main action. The soundscape should not drop to absolute silence between words or impacts unless silence itself is specifically part of the scene. A slight breath, distant water, an electrical hum or air moving through leaves can make the pause feel inhabited. Keep that bed modest and stable. It should not swell dramatically to fill every gap, and it should not become a musical drone unless music is explicitly called for in the scene description.

When music is absent, let rhythm come from the physical activity and environment. Repeated tools, footsteps, water or machinery may produce patterns without turning into an added score. When the scene contains musicians, their audible performance belongs to their visible instruments and gestures. Keep the number and character of those instruments consistent with the performers present. A lingering resonance after a final beat is natural; a new unseen orchestra entering at the ending is not. Preserve the scene's chosen distinction between performed sound and background music.

The overall mix should allow the viewer to understand what is happening without sounding artificially isolated. A voice can sit above a moderate environmental bed while still sharing its space. A loud brief action can mask quieter details for an instant, then allow them to emerge again. Do not hold a background effect at an unnaturally fixed level through every foreground event. Keep abrupt level changes tied to a plausible source, such as a motor stopping or a door opening. The final tail should retain the acoustic character established at the beginning.

If the scene contains a cut, carry through the physical state of the event. The object held before the cut remains in the same hand afterward. A person does not change clothes or reverse their place in the setting. An ongoing sound can bridge the cut and help connect the views. A closer shot reveals detail that was already part of the wider scene rather than introducing a new version of the object. The cut should clarify the described action, and the sequence should contain only the views specified in the scene brief.

Keep screen direction understandable across those views. If two people face one another, retain their established left-right relationship unless the camera visibly crosses to the other side. A moving object that exits toward one side should enter the next view in a direction consistent with the shared space. The viewer should not need an unexplained reversal to understand a simple handover, gesture or tool movement. A small change in angle is enough to reveal depth; it does not require the entire setting to rotate around the subjects.

At the beginning, the event can already be underway. A wheel may already be turning, water already moving, or a musician already holding the position needed for the next beat. Establish enough context in the first image that the main action is immediately legible. Avoid spending most of the clip on an empty establishing view if the scene asks for a brief exchange or quick movement. The setting's detail should be available within the composition while the event proceeds, rather than delaying the event until every object has been introduced.

At the ending, let the action reach its stated small resolution. A received object rests in the new holder's hand; a final beat resonates; a returning animal settles; a tool stops where the worker intends it to stop. The environment can continue moving beyond that resolution. Hold the final relationship long enough to read it without adding a second incident. There is no need for a fade, a logo, an explanatory caption or a dramatic new camera move. End with the same world still present around the subjects.

The emotional tone should arise from the described behavior. Quiet concentration can be conveyed through deliberate attention and economical movement. Enjoyment can appear in a brief shared glance or a relaxed recovery after a successful action. A natural event can be compelling without making the animal perform a human expression. Avoid enlarging a restrained scene into a melodramatic confrontation or celebration. The viewer should feel close to an ordinary moment whose textures, sounds and physical details make it worth watching.

Use background detail to support this tone. A working place may show wear, repairs, stored tools and traces of repeated use, but it need not become dirty or neglected. A natural place can include uneven growth and debris without looking arranged for decoration. An organized surface can still have small asymmetries. Let these details belong to the activity and location rather than scattering unrelated objects to fill empty space. A few clear relationships between the people, their work and the setting are more valuable than a frame in which every surface competes for attention.

Keep the image free from unexplained graphic effects. Light can flare modestly when a bright source approaches the lens, but it should not produce decorative streaks unrelated to that source. A shallow depth of field can soften a background without cutting the subject out against a featureless wash. Grain, if visible, remains fine and unobtrusive. The scene's realism comes from consistent observation of light, material, movement and sound; it does not require an artificial cinematic overlay or a conspicuous filter announcing a style separate from the event.

Throughout the clip, preserve the hierarchy of attention established by the scene. The main action is clearest, the immediate surroundings explain it, and the more distant setting gives it a place in the world. A small incidental detail can become briefly noticeable when light or motion reveals it, then return to the background. Do not let incidental motion interrupt the only spoken line or conceal the decisive gesture. All of the detailed directions belong to this one hierarchy and should combine into a readable, continuous experience.

Consider the space just beyond each edge of the picture as a continuation of the visible setting. A wall that exits the frame still explains the light reflected onto a nearby face. A stream entering from the distance carries water from a place beyond the composition. A work surface continues far enough to support the tools partly hidden at its edge. Do not bend these surfaces toward the lens simply to show every named detail. Partial visibility is natural when the camera occupies one definite position. The scene remains understandable because the visible portions agree with one another.

Where the foreground and background have similar colors, separate them through ordinary optical cues. A near edge may be sharper, a distant surface may receive softer light, and a contact shadow may reveal a small gap. Do not draw an artificial bright outline around the subject. If two dark forms overlap, allow some of that overlap to remain quiet rather than forcing every boundary to maximum contrast. The most important contacts need clarity; less important background junctions can be ambiguous in the normal way a photographed place is ambiguous when viewed briefly.

Use negative space as part of the action's readability. A hand needs a little clear area in the direction it is about to move, and a diving bird needs visible water below its perch. This does not mean centering every object or distributing props symmetrically. The free space should follow the scene's physical task. When the action occupies that space, the composition can become denser for a moment and then open again during recovery. Preserve the setting behind it so this change feels like movement through a place rather than a rearrangement of graphic elements.

The brightest region of the frame need not always be the protagonist. A window, sky opening or lamp can be brighter while the subject remains readable through shape, placement and movement. Control the size of those bright regions so they do not overwhelm the action. A small reflection can attract the eye briefly and then recede as its angle changes. Broad highlights should retain enough shape to identify their source. Avoid automatic-looking exposure pumping that darkens a face whenever a reflective object passes through the composition.

Let shadows have different degrees of softness according to the light source and distance to the receiving surface. A broad window gives a soft edge, while a small nearby lamp can make a more defined one. Contact shadows are tighter than shadows cast across a larger gap. On a curved receiver, the shadow changes shape with the surface. If several sources contribute, their shadows can overlap with unequal darkness. This physical variety helps explain depth without applying the same dark border to every object in the scene.

Reflected color should be local. A warm wooden counter can return a little warmth to the underside of a hand above it. Green foliage can tint a shaded patch near a stream. Red task lighting can color nearby metal while a distant opening remains cool. Keep these influences weaker than direct illumination unless the scene explicitly places the subject close to a large colored surface. The reflected tint belongs to the facing side of an object and changes gradually with distance, rather than replacing the object's base color across its whole visible area.

Fine details should obey the scale of the view. A close hand can show a crease near a knuckle, while a distant face needs only its stable overall proportions and expression. The grain of a nearby wooden tool may be visible, but the far wall does not need equally sharp pores. As the camera moves, details become available through a change of angle or distance rather than appearing as random new marks. Preserve the few distinctive features that identify an object, and let less important texture merge naturally when it is too small to resolve.

When a reflective surface turns, its highlight can travel faster than a mark painted on it. The highlight is an image of a light source seen through the changing surface orientation, while the mark belongs to the material. Keep that distinction on wet clay, metal rims, glass and water. A highlight may disappear at an angle where the source is no longer reflected. It should not leave a permanent bright stain behind. Conversely, a scratch or chip does not slide across the object merely because the camera changes position.

Translucent edges should retain their own thickness and texture. A leaf against the light may show a warmer or brighter rim, and thin paper can glow softly near a lamp. Steam can reveal an object behind it with reduced contrast. These are different kinds of transmission and should not share one identical blur effect. Keep opaque supports opaque. A hand behind steam remains a solid hand glimpsed through moving vapor; it does not become transparent itself. When the vapor clears, the same hand occupies the same physical place.

The action's pace should fit ordinary human or animal movement at its stated scale. A small precise adjustment takes less travel than a broad gesture but can still have a deliberate beginning and ending. Do not stretch a short line of dialogue into unnatural syllables merely to occupy the clip. Let quiet anticipation and recovery provide breathing room where appropriate. A fast event can happen within a calmer surrounding interval. The scene's detail describes what is present during that interval, rather than requiring each noun to receive its own separate time allocation.

For scenes with speech, give the sentence an intention appropriate to the relationship. A practical instruction can be clear without being stern; a question can be curious without sounding anxious; a calm reply can be warm without becoming sentimental. Preserve the exact specified words while allowing natural stress, breath and a small pause where the phrase requires it. The listener's response begins from understanding the line, not from mechanically copying the speaker's mouth. Environmental sound continues underneath, maintaining the shared location of both people.

In scenes without speech, resist the urge to supply explanatory vocalizations. The sound of an instrument, water, machinery or food cooking can make the action clear. Silence from the visible subjects is compatible with a detailed active environment. A small breath or ordinary incidental sound may belong naturally to a person, but it should not become a substitute narration. The listener should be able to follow the relationship between motion and sound without being told what to notice by an unseen voice.

Maintain the distinction between a source moving and a listener moving. During a small camera track, a stationary sound source can shift gently within the stereo field because the viewpoint changes. It does not need to grow suddenly louder unless the camera actually approaches it substantially. A moving source can change position while the environmental bed remains stable. Keep those changes smooth and proportional to the visible distances. Avoid a dramatic stereo sweep for an object that moves only a few centimeters within the shot.

Low sounds should carry weight without masking every other detail. A motor hum or drum body can occupy the lower register while speech, contact sounds and air movement remain distinguishable above it. The low component should not become a continuous cinematic rumble added to an otherwise ordinary place. High sounds likewise need restraint: metallic jingles, a relay click or water spray can be crisp without piercing the mix. The balance should resemble attentive listening near the scene, with enough dynamic room for a meaningful brief event to stand out.

The last audible decay can continue after the corresponding visible motion stops. A resonant instrument, hard-surface tap or small splash leaves a short tail in its environment. That tail should gradually reveal the quieter background already present underneath. Do not cut every source off at the same instant or replace the ending with a generic fade. The world continues beyond the chosen end of the observation. Let the final image and sound preserve that sense of an ongoing place while the main action reaches its stated resolution.
